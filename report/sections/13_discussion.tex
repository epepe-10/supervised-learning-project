\section{Alternatives tested, limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{An alternative we tested: a ``missing'' category}

The analysis left an open question (Section~\ref{sec:eda}): are the missing job
values random? On the development set, the 2{,}233 people with a missing
\code{workclass} all have a missing \code{occupation} too (2{,}241 missing
occupations in total: the other 8 people never worked), and only 9.4\% of the
people with a missing occupation earn \pos, against 23.9\% overall. They are mostly people without a job.
The most-frequent imputation hides this, since it gives them the most common
job (\code{Private}, \code{Prof-specialty}). Imputing a separate ``missing''
category keeps the information. We measured its effect on the untuned models
(Table~\ref{tab:missing}).

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Most-frequent imputation against a ``missing'' category for the
categorical columns (untuned models, 5-fold out-of-fold, development set).}
\label{tab:missing}
\begin{tabular}{llrrrr}
\toprule
Model & Imputation & AP & AP std & ROC-AUC & F1 (tuned thr.) \\
\midrule
Logistic regression & Most frequent & 0.7839 & 0.0032 & 0.9125 & 0.6992 \\
                    & ``missing''   & 0.7864 & 0.0039 & 0.9136 & 0.7013 \\
\midrule
LightGBM            & Most frequent & 0.8307 & 0.0014 & 0.9289 & 0.7314 \\
                    & ``missing''   & 0.8318 & 0.0020 & 0.9295 & 0.7313 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The gain is 0.003 AP for the logistic regression and 0.001 for LightGBM, with
no change in F1 for LightGBM: of the order of the variation of AP across folds.
Adopting it would have meant re-running the whole tuning for no measurable
gain, so we kept the most-frequent imputation. A separate category remains the
more principled choice for a model in production, because it does not invent a
job for people who have none.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Old data.} The census dates from 1994. Incomes, jobs and the
  meaning of the \$50K threshold have changed since, so the model would need to
  be retrained on recent data before any use.
  \item \textbf{Possible leakage.} \code{capital-gain} and \code{capital-loss}
  are declared for the same tax year as the income (Section~\ref{sec:eda}).
  \item \textbf{F1 stands in for business costs.} With real costs for a false
  positive and a false negative, the threshold should minimize the expected
  cost instead.
  \item \textbf{Threshold chosen on fold models.} The threshold comes from the
  out-of-fold probabilities of five models trained on 80\% of the development
  set, and is applied to a model refitted on all of it. Their scores are close,
  but not identical.
  \item \textbf{Probabilities not calibrated} as-is
  (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).
  \item \textbf{No fairness audit.} The model uses \code{sex} and \code{race}
  as inputs, and the share of \pos{} is 30\% for men against 11\% for women in
  the data. The fairness audit was removed from the scope, so we did not check
  whether errors are spread equally across groups; this would be needed before
  any real use.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion}

Our final model is a tuned LightGBM inside a single scikit-learn Pipeline. On
the untouched test set it reaches an AP of 0.821 and an F1 of 0.722 at its
frozen threshold of 0.54, within 0.01 of its cross-validation scores. What
mattered most was the choice of model (LightGBM is about 0.05 AP ahead of the
logistic regression), then the decision threshold, then the engineered
features. Its decisions are reliable, but its raw probabilities are
over-estimated because of the class weight, so they should be recalibrated
before being read as true chances (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).
