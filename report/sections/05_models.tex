\section{Modelling progression}
\label{sec:models}

We compared three models of increasing flexibility, inside the same Pipeline,
on the same folds and with the same metrics.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Logistic regression}: linear in the log-odds, interpretable and
  probabilistic, which makes it a strong baseline. The \code{saga} solver
  supports the three penalties: \code{l1\_ratio} sets the mix (0 = Ridge, 1 =
  Lasso, in between = Elastic Net) and $C = 1/\lambda$ its strength.
  \item \textbf{Random forest}: many deep trees, each trained on a bootstrap
  sample with a random subset of features at each split, then averaged.
  Averaging decorrelated trees reduces the variance, and trees capture
  non-linear effects and interactions without feature engineering.
  \item \textbf{LightGBM}: gradient boosting. Trees are added one at a time,
  each one correcting the errors of the current ensemble, and grown leaf-wise.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The logistic regression in one equation.} The model computes a
linear score and turns it into a probability with the sigmoid:
\[
  p(x) = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad
  z = \beta_0 + \sum_j \beta_j x_j = \log\frac{p}{1-p}.
\]
It is linear in the log-odds, not in the probability, so its decision boundary
$z = \text{const}$ is a hyperplane in the feature space: curves, thresholds and
interactions must be given to it as features (Section~\ref{sec:features}). The
coefficients minimize the log-loss (the negative log-likelihood) plus the
Elastic Net penalty:
\[
  \min_\beta \; -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \bigl[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\bigr]
  + \lambda \Bigl[\alpha \lVert\beta\rVert_1 + \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2\Bigr],
\]
with $\alpha$ = \code{l1\_ratio} and $\lambda = 1/C$ (scikit-learn writes the
same trade-off with $C$ in front of the loss). A coefficient adds $\beta_j$ to
the log-odds, i.e.\ multiplies the odds by $e^{\beta_j}$, other columns held
fixed. Since the one-hot encoding keeps both \code{sex} columns, the effect of
being a woman rather than a man is the difference of the two coefficients:
$-1.87 - (-1.13) = -0.74$, so the odds of \pos{} are multiplied by
$e^{-0.74} \approx 0.48$ at equal age, education, job and hours. The effect is
constant on the odds, not on the probability, because the sigmoid is not
linear.

\paragraph{How a tree chooses its splits.} A node is \emph{pure} when all its
rows belong to the same class. Impurity measures how far a node is from that.
With $p$ the share of >50K in the node, the two usual measures are
\[
  \text{Gini} = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2 = 2p(1-p),
  \qquad
  \text{Entropy} = -\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k .
\]
Both are 0 for a pure node and largest at $p = 0.5$. At the root of our trees,
$p = 0.24$, so Gini $= 0.365$ and entropy $= 0.795$ bits. At each node, the tree
tries the candidate splits and keeps the one that most reduces the impurity,
weighted by the number of rows in each child (with entropy, this reduction is
the \emph{information gain}). The two measures almost always pick the same
splits. We kept scikit-learn's default, Gini, which is faster because it has
no logarithm, and did not tune it. Left alone, the tree keeps splitting until
every leaf is pure, i.e.\ until it has memorized its training rows. This is why
the forest's \code{max\_depth} and \code{min\_samples\_leaf} matter
(Section~\ref{sec:tuning}). LightGBM does not use an impurity measure. It
picks the split that most reduces the log-loss, computed from the gradients of
the loss, and its L2 penalty \code{reg\_lambda} enters that gain directly.

\paragraph{How boosting corrects its errors.} LightGBM builds an additive model
on the log-odds scale. It starts from a constant $F_0$ (the log-odds of the
base rate), then, at each round $m$, fits a small tree $T_m$ to the negative
gradient of the log-loss with respect to the current score, which is simply
$r_i = y_i - p_i$: positive when the model underestimates a \pos{} person,
negative when it overestimates a \negc{} one. The tree is added with a learning
rate $\eta$:
\[
  F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \eta\, T_m(x), \qquad
  p(x) = \sigma\bigl(F_M(x)\bigr).
\]
A small $\eta$ makes each correction cautious and needs more trees, so the two
are tuned together; our tuned model uses $\eta = 0.048$ and 350 trees
(Section~\ref{sec:tuning}). The two ensembles attack different errors: the
random forest averages independent deep trees, which mainly reduces the
variance, while boosting adds corrections one after the other, which mainly
reduces the bias but can overfit if there are too many or too complex trees.
LightGBM, XGBoost and CatBoost all implement this same algorithm with
different engineering choices. We used LightGBM, the course's working model,
because it is the fastest of the three, which matters when each of the 50
tuning trials means five fits. CatBoost's main strength, high-cardinality
categorical features, does not apply here: our largest categorical column,
\code{native-country}, has about forty categories.

\paragraph{What each step bought.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Feature engineering} added 0.021 AP to the logistic regression
  (Section~\ref{sec:features}).
  \item \textbf{The untuned random forest is worse than the logistic
  regression} (AP 0.750 against 0.784, log-loss 0.446). With the default
  settings, the trees grow until each leaf is pure: they memorize the training
  folds, and the averaged probabilities are poorly calibrated. The forest needs
  its complexity to be tuned (depth, leaf size), which Section~\ref{sec:tuning}
  does.
  \item \textbf{LightGBM is the best model on every metric}: AP 0.831 (+0.047
  over the logistic regression), ROC-AUC 0.929, log-loss 0.275 and F1 0.731.
  The gaps between models (at least 0.03 AP) are about ten times larger than
  the variation of AP across folds (at most 0.006), so the ranking is not an
  artefact of the split.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What tuning bought.} Tuning mattered most where the defaults were
worst. The random forest gains 0.064 AP (0.750 to 0.814) and 0.038 F1 once its
depth and leaf size are limited: it stops memorizing the training folds and
moves from last to second place. This is the bias--variance trade-off of the
course: a deep tree with tiny leaves has low bias but high variance, a shallow
one the opposite, and the tuned depth of 14 with at least 3 rows per leaf is
the compromise chosen by cross-validation. The logistic regression does not move (AP
0.784, F1 0.699): with only three hyperparameters and a linear decision
boundary, the default settings were already close to its best. LightGBM gains
almost nothing (AP 0.831 to 0.832), because its defaults were already well
suited to this table. The ranking of the tuned models is the same on every
metric except recall, where the balanced random forest is ahead.

The final model is the one with the best out-of-fold AP after tuning:
LightGBM. It is chosen on cross-validation only, before the
test set is used.
