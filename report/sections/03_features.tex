\section{Feature engineering and preprocessing}
\label{sec:features}

\subsection{Three engineered features}

A logistic regression is linear in the log-odds: it cannot draw a curve, a
threshold or an interaction on its own. We gave it these shapes explicitly,
with one feature of each kind, each motivated by the analysis above.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{age\_squared} $= \text{age}^2$ (square term). With \code{age} and
  \code{age\_squared}, the model can draw the inverted U of
  Figure~\ref{fig:eda-age} instead of a straight line.
  \item \code{education\_x\_hours} $= \text{education-num} \times
  \text{hours-per-week}$ (interaction). The value of an extra hour of work is
  likely to depend on the level of education; the product lets the effect of
  one feature depend on the other.
  \item \code{has\_capital} $= \mathbf{1}[\text{capital-gain} > 0 \text{ or }
  \text{capital-loss} > 0]$ (indicator). With more than 90\% of the amounts at
  zero, owning capital at all is a cleaner signal than the raw, very skewed
  amounts.
\end{itemize}
The three features only combine columns of the same row. They learn nothing
from the data (no mean, no median, no target), so they cannot leak information
from validation rows into training rows. They are computed inside the Pipeline,
so they are recomputed for any new row the saved model scores.

\paragraph{What they bought.} On the untuned logistic regression (5-fold
out-of-fold predictions, threshold 0.5), the three features raise ROC-AUC from
0.905 to 0.913, AP from 0.763 to 0.784 and F1 from 0.657 to 0.673. This gain is
about seven times the standard deviation of AP across folds (0.003). Tree models
can learn such shapes by themselves, so we expected the features to matter
mostly for the linear model; we kept them for all three models so that every
model sees the same inputs. SHAP shows that LightGBM does use two of them
(Section~\ref{sec:shap}): \code{education\_x\_hours} is its 5th most important
feature, ahead of \code{education-num}, and \code{age\_squared} is 8th.
\code{has\_capital} matters little (12th of 15), since the trees can split on
\code{capital-gain} directly.

Note that \code{age} and \code{age\_squared} are strongly correlated. With a
regularized model this does not hurt the predictions, but the two features
share the credit, so their coefficients and their SHAP values must be read
together.

\subsection{Missing values and encoding}

All the steps below are learned on the training rows only, inside each
cross-validation fold (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Categorical columns} (7): missing values are replaced by the
  most frequent category of the training rows, then one-hot encoded: categories
  are labels, not measurements, so each one gets its own column.
  \code{handle\_unknown="ignore"} turns a category never seen in training into
  a row of zeros instead of an error at prediction time.
  \item \textbf{Numerical columns} (5 original and 3 engineered): missing values
  are replaced by the training median, then standardized, $x \mapsto (x -
  \mu)/\sigma$ with the training mean and standard deviation, which matters for
  the regularized logistic regression. Adult has no missing numerical value,
  but the imputer stays so that a new row with a missing number can still be
  scored.
\end{itemize}
After encoding, the model sees 91 columns (when fitted on the whole development
set). Imputing the most frequent category is the simplest choice;
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} shows that the missing job values are not random
and tests a separate ``missing'' category instead.
