\section{Explainability with SHAP}
\label{sec:shap}

\paragraph{Method.} SHAP splits one prediction into a reference value plus one
contribution per feature: $f(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_j \phi_j(x)$. For LightGBM,
\code{TreeExplainer} computes these values exactly, in log-odds of \pos. We
explain the final model on 1{,}000 people drawn from the development set (the
test set stays untouched). SHAP values are additive, so we sum the values of
the one-hot columns of each categorical feature to get one contribution per
original feature. As a check, for every explained person, the base value plus
the contributions gives back the raw score, and its sigmoid the predicted
probability.

\paragraph{Global importance.} The global importance of a feature is its mean
absolute SHAP value (Figure~\ref{fig:shap-global}). Marital status comes first
by far (0.93 in log-odds), followed by age (0.58), capital gains (0.53),
relationship (0.41) and our interaction \code{education\_x\_hours} (0.39);
occupation (0.31) and \code{education-num} (0.30) come next. Sex is 9th (0.18)
and race 14th (0.06) out of 15. The beeswarm adds the direction: a large
capital gain pushes the score up by $+4$ to $+7$, the largest single effects of
the model, and young ages pull it down (to about $-2.5$).

\paragraph{Local explanations.} We explain three people, all starting from the
base value $\phi_0 = -1.93$ (a probability of 0.13; waterfall plots in
Figure~\ref{fig:shap-local}, appendix). The most confident \pos{} prediction
($P = 0.999$, truly \pos) owes $+5.67$ of its $+9.26$ to a capital gain of
\$15{,}024, the rest to marriage, 16 years of education and an executive job.
The most confident \negc{} ($P < 0.001$, truly \negc) is mainly explained by
age 18 ($-2.17$ for \code{age}, $-0.79$ for \code{age\_squared}). The person
closest to the threshold ($P = 0.539$, just below 0.54, truly \negc) is a
married man of 35: marriage ($+0.88$) and a high \code{education\_x\_hours}
($+0.63$) push up, but the absence of capital gains ($-0.21$) keeps him under
the threshold.

\paragraph{Do the important features make business sense?} Mostly yes. The
household variables come first (marital status and relationship together weigh
1.34): being married is a proxy for age, career stage and a stable household.
Capital gains are a direct sign of wealth, and age, education and occupation
describe a career. Our engineered features are used: \code{education\_x\_hours}
is 5th, ahead of \code{education-num}, and \code{age\_squared} 8th, while
\code{has\_capital} is only 12th because the trees can split on
\code{capital-gain} directly. Sex weighs less, but not zero: being a woman
lowers the score by about $0.3$ in log-odds, on top of what passes through
\code{relationship}. The model reproduces the income gap between men and women
in the 1994 census; with the fairness audit out of scope, we flag it as a
limitation. Two cautions apply: SHAP explains what the model does, not what
causes a high income, and correlated features (\code{age} and
\code{age\_squared}, \code{marital-status} and \code{relationship}) share the
credit and must be read together.
