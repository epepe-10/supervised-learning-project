\section{Calibration diagnostics}
\label{sec:calibration}

Ranking and calibration are two different questions. AP and ROC-AUC ask
whether \pos{} people get higher scores than the others; calibration asks
whether a predicted 0.7 means that about 70\% of such people really earn \pos.

\paragraph{Method.} We judge the final model on its out-of-fold probabilities
on the development set: each person is predicted by a model that did not train
on them, and the test set stays untouched. The reliability diagram groups the
predictions into 10 equal-width bins and compares, in each bin, the mean
predicted probability with the observed share of \pos. Two scores complete it
(lower is better): the \textbf{Brier score}, the mean squared error between the
predicted probability and the 0/1 outcome (always predicting the prevalence
gives $0.239 \times 0.761 = 0.182$), and the \textbf{expected calibration
error}, $\text{ECE} = \sum_m \frac{|B_m|}{n}\,\lvert \text{acc}(B_m) -
\text{conf}(B_m) \rvert$, the gap between observed share and mean prediction in
each bin $B_m$, weighted by its share of people.

\paragraph{Results.} On the 39{,}032 out-of-fold predictions of LightGBM, the
Brier score is 0.092, half of the 0.182 of the constant prediction, and the ECE
is 0.049 (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}; per-bin values in
Table~\ref{tab:bins}, appendix). Below 0.1, where half of the people fall, the
model is nearly calibrated; around 0.55, it predicts 55\% where only 39\% earn
\pos. The mean prediction is 0.289 against an observed share of 0.239.

\paragraph{Over- or under-confident?} The curve lies below the diagonal, with a
positive gap in all ten bins (from $+0.005$ to $+0.159$): the model is
over-confident in its \pos{} predictions, most of all in the middle of the
range.

\paragraph{Why: the class weight.} Optuna kept \code{scale\_pos\_weight}
$= 1.80$ for LightGBM, which makes each \pos{} person count more during
training and shifts all the probabilities up. To check this, we compared two
untuned LightGBM models that differ only by this weight (Table~\ref{tab:spw}).

The weight changes neither the ranking (same AP) nor the decisions (same F1),
but it multiplies the ECE by eleven: AP only depends on the order of the
scores, so Optuna had no reason to prefer one weight over another.

\paragraph{Would we trust the probabilities as-is?} For the decisions, yes: the
threshold was chosen on these same, shifted probabilities, so the \pos{} /
\negc{} decisions are consistent. For the probabilities themselves, no: any
downstream use that reads them as real chances would over-estimate. Rather
than recalibrating after training (Platt scaling or isotonic regression), we
would fix \code{scale\_pos\_weight} to 1: it costs nothing in ranking or
decisions and brings the ECE from 0.051 to 0.005 (Table~\ref{tab:spw}). We did
not apply it because the test set had already been used
(Section~\ref{sec:test}): changing the model afterwards would have required a
second look at it.
