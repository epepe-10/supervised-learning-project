\section{Calibration diagnostics}
\label{sec:calibration}

Ranking and calibration are two different questions. AP and ROC-AUC ask
whether \pos{} people get higher scores than the others; calibration asks
whether a predicted 0.7 means that about 70\% of such people really earn \pos.

\paragraph{Method.} We judge the final model on its out-of-fold probabilities
on the development set: each person is predicted by a model that did not train
on them, and the test set stays untouched. The reliability diagram groups the
predictions into 10 equal-width bins and compares, in each bin, the mean
predicted probability with the observed share of \pos; a histogram next to it
shows how many people fall in each bin. Two summary scores complete it (lower
is better for both):
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \textbf{Brier score}, the mean squared error between the predicted
  probability and the 0/1 outcome. As a reference, always predicting the
  prevalence gives $0.239 \times 0.761 = 0.182$;
  \item the \textbf{expected calibration error},
  $\text{ECE} = \sum_m \frac{|B_m|}{n}\,\lvert \text{acc}(B_m) -
  \text{conf}(B_m) \rvert$: the gap between observed share and mean prediction
  in each bin $B_m$, weighted by the share of people in that bin.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Results.} On the 39{,}032 out-of-fold predictions of LightGBM, the
Brier score is 0.092, half of the 0.182 of the constant prediction, and the ECE
is 0.049 (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}, Table~\ref{tab:bins}). The histogram
shows that half of the people (19{,}171) get a probability below 0.1, where the
model is nearly calibrated. The gaps are largest in the middle of the range:
around 0.55, the model predicts 55\% where only 39\% of the people earn \pos.
Over all the people, the mean prediction is 0.289 against an observed share of
0.239.

\paragraph{Over- or under-confident?} The curve lies below the diagonal: in each
bin, the model predicts a higher probability of \pos{} than the share
observed. The gap is positive in all ten bins of Table~\ref{tab:bins}, from
$+0.005$ at the top of the range to $+0.159$ around 0.55. The model systematically over-estimates the chance of \pos, most of
all in the middle of the range: it is over-confident in its \pos{}
predictions.

\paragraph{Why: the class weight.} Optuna kept \code{scale\_pos\_weight}
$= 1.80$ for LightGBM. A class weight makes each \pos{}
person count more during training, which shifts all the probabilities up. To
check that this is the cause, we compared two untuned LightGBM models that
differ only by this weight (Table~\ref{tab:spw}).

\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The weight changes neither the ranking (same AP) nor the decisions (same F1,
once the threshold is tuned), but it multiplies the ECE by eleven and raises
the mean prediction from 0.239 to 0.291. This is consistent with
Section~\ref{sec:imbalance}: AP only depends on the order of the scores, so
Optuna had no reason to prefer one weight over another, and the tuned
threshold already handles the imbalance.

\paragraph{Would we trust the probabilities as-is?} For the decisions, yes: the
threshold was chosen on these same, shifted probabilities, so the \pos{} /
\negc{} decisions are consistent. For the probabilities themselves, no: any
downstream use that reads them as real chances (an expected number of \pos{}
people, a score shown to a user) would over-estimate. Two fixes are possible:
set \code{scale\_pos\_weight} to 1 and tune again, or recalibrate the
probabilities after training (Platt scaling or isotonic regression, fitted on
out-of-fold predictions). \todo{team decision: say which fix we would apply,
or apply one.}
